Bad chunking is the silent killer of RAG pipelines. The model, the embeddings, the vector database — none of that matters if the chunks you retrieve are too big, too small, or split at the wrong places.

## Why chunking matters

Most embedding models have a token limit. Documents are almost always longer than that. Chunking is how you break them into pieces that fit.

But it's not just about fitting. The **chunk you retrieve is the chunk the model reads**. If a chunk buries the relevant sentence in three paragraphs of surrounding noise, the model may still miss it. If a chunk is a single sentence stripped of its context — "It was introduced to address this problem." — the model has no idea what "it" refers to.

Every chunking decision is a tradeoff between two failure modes:

- **Too large**: noisier chunks, lower retrieval precision, bloated prompts
- **Too small**: lost context, broken sentences, retrieval that scores fragments rather than ideas

## The main chunking strategies

### 1. Fixed-size chunking

Split every N tokens, with optional overlap between consecutive chunks.

```text
chunk_size = 512 tokens
overlap    = 64 tokens   # last 64 tokens of chunk N repeat at the start of chunk N+1
```

This is the default in most tutorials because it's easy to implement and works well on clean, uniform text — product docs, knowledge bases, wiki pages.

**When it breaks**: when sentences span chunk boundaries, or when each paragraph is a self-contained idea that a fixed window always splits in half.

### 2. Sentence or paragraph splitting

Split on natural language boundaries — periods, newlines, blank lines — rather than by token count.

The chunks align with how people actually write, so embedding quality tends to be better. The downside: chunk length is unpredictable, and some chunks may be too long for your embedding model while others are nearly meaningless on their own.

A common fix is a **two-pass approach**: split by sentence, then group adjacent sentences until you hit a token budget.

### 3. Recursive character splitting

A middle path: try splitting on paragraphs first (`\n\n`), then sentences, then spaces — shrinking the separator until you get chunks under your target size. This is the strategy behind tools like LangChain's `RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter`.

Works well on mixed-format text where you don't know the structure in advance.

### 4. Semantic chunking

Embed each sentence, then group sentences that are close in meaning and start a new chunk when the topic shifts.

This produces chunks that align with **meaning** rather than arbitrary boundaries. Retrieval precision can improve noticeably on heterogeneous documents — a long PDF that covers five different topics, for instance.

The tradeoff: slower to index, more moving parts to debug.

### 5. Structure-aware chunking

If your documents have clear structure — headers, sections, code blocks — honor it. Split on `##` headings, keep the heading text inside the chunk, and treat each section as its own unit.

This is the right move when documents are structured and that structure is informative: technical reference manuals, API docs, policy documents. The section boundaries are usually the most meaningful splits.

## Choosing a strategy

| Strategy | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed-size | Uniform prose, fast to ship | Splits sentences mid-thought |
| Sentence / paragraph | Clean, well-written text | Unpredictable chunk lengths |
| Recursive splitting | Mixed-format documents | Needs configuration tuning |
| Semantic | Long, topically varied docs | Slower indexing |
| Structure-aware | Docs with clear headers | Requires parsing the structure |

## The overlap question

Almost every strategy benefits from **chunk overlap** — repeating a small number of tokens from the end of one chunk at the start of the next. It prevents the retrieval system from missing a sentence that happens to straddle a boundary.

A reasonable default is 10–20% of the chunk size. More than that and you're embedding redundant content; less and you risk dropped context at every seam.

> Rule of thumb: **start with fixed-size plus overlap, evaluate retrieval quality, then switch strategies only when you can measure the improvement.** Don't optimize chunking before you have a way to score it.

## How to tell if your chunks are hurting you

Chunking failures show up as retrieval failures: the right information exists in your corpus, but the retrieved chunks don't contain it — or contain it buried in noise. To catch this:

1. **Inspect chunks manually.** Pull a random sample and read them. Could you answer a question using only this text?
2. **Build a small eval set.** Write 20 questions whose answers you know. See how often the correct chunk appears in the top three results.
3. **Check similarity scores.** Low scores between a query and the chunk you'd expect to match often point to a chunking mismatch — the chunk text doesn't resemble how users phrase questions.

If you're seeing answers that miss the point despite the right document being in your corpus, start with chunking and retrieval before you look at the model.
