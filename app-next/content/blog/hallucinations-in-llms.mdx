LLMs don't lie — they don't even *know* they're wrong. When a model tells you a paper was published in 2019 by a researcher who doesn't exist, it's doing exactly what it was trained to do: predict plausible-sounding text. The problem is that "plausible" and "true" aren't the same thing.

That gap — confident, fluent output that isn't grounded in fact — is what we call a **hallucination**.

## What is a hallucination?

A hallucination is any output from an LLM that is **factually incorrect, unsupported by context, or entirely fabricated** — delivered with the same confident tone the model uses when it's right.

This isn't a bug that will simply be patched away. It's a consequence of how language models work: they predict the next token based on patterns in training data. They're very good at producing text that *reads* like a correct answer, but they have no internal fact-checker validating what they generate.

## Why do LLMs hallucinate?

Several root causes are at play:

- **Knowledge gaps** — The model was trained on data with a cutoff date, so anything after that point is simply unknown territory.
- **Low-frequency facts** — Niche or obscure facts appeared rarely in training data, so the model has a weak grip on them and fills in details.
- **Overfitted fluency** — Models are trained to produce coherent, well-formed text. That reward signal doesn't distinguish between truthful and plausible.
- **No grounding** — Without retrieval, the model generates from patterns. There's nothing anchoring it to verified facts.

> The model isn't looking things up. It's constructing an answer that fits the shape of the question.

## Two types you need to know

Hallucinations come in two forms, and the distinction matters for which fix to reach for:

| Type | What it is | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Factual hallucination** | The model invents a fact not in its training data | Citing a paper with a real-sounding title that doesn't exist |
| **Faithfulness hallucination** | The model contradicts or ignores context it was explicitly given | Summarizing a document and changing a number or name from the source |

Factual hallucinations are the ones most people hear about. Faithfulness hallucinations are arguably worse for production systems — the model is ignoring information you handed it. The fix for each is different, so naming them is useful.

## Practical ways to reduce hallucinations

You can't eliminate hallucinations entirely, but you can reduce them significantly.

**1. Ground the model with retrieval**

The single most effective technique is to give the model the facts, then ask it to answer *from that context only*. This is [retrieval-augmented generation](/blog/what-is-rag). When the model has a source document in front of it and is instructed to stay within it, faithfulness hallucinations drop substantially. The model isn't relying on memory — it's reading.

**2. Constrain the output format**

Asking for structured output — a JSON object, a list of bullet points, a table with citations — forces the model to organize rather than free-form. Structure creates natural checkpoints where fabrication is easier to spot and to test.

**3. Write grounding instructions into the prompt**

Explicit instructions like *"If you are not sure, say you don't know"* or *"Only use information from the provided context — do not add outside knowledge"* do help, especially for faithfulness errors. This isn't a complete solution, but it moves the needle.

**4. Add a verification step**

For high-stakes outputs, treat the first generation as a draft. Run a second prompt asking the model to check each factual claim in the draft against the source documents. This two-step pattern catches a large share of faithfulness errors:

```text
Step 1: Generate answer given source documents

Step 2: "Check each factual claim in the answer against the
         documents. Flag any claim not directly supported."
```

This is an example of a simple [workflow rather than an agent](/blog/agents-vs-workflows) — fixed steps, predictable, easy to test.

**5. Evaluate systematically**

None of these techniques matter if you don't measure their effect. Build an evaluation set — real questions with known answers — and score your pipeline before and after each change. Without evals, you won't know whether you've actually improved anything or just moved the problem.

## When hallucinations matter most

Not every hallucination is catastrophic. A creative brainstorming tool can tolerate a certain amount of invention. A medical summary, a legal brief, or a support bot answering questions from your documentation cannot.

The higher the stakes of an incorrect answer, the more you need to invest in grounding, verification, and evals. The pattern is consistent: hallucinations are most frequent when the model is asked to recall obscure facts without any retrieval, and far less frequent when you hand it the right source material and constrain it to use that material.
