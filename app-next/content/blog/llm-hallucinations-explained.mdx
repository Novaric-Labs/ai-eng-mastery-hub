There's a specific kind of AI failure that trips people up the first time they see it: the model gives a fluent, confident, well-formatted answer that is completely wrong. No disclaimer. No "I'm not sure." Just wrong — with authority.

This is called a **hallucination**, and once you understand *why* it happens, you can build systems that make it far less likely.

## What is a hallucination?

In the context of LLMs, a hallucination is any output that is factually incorrect, fabricated, or unsupported by the input — yet stated as if it were true.

The word is borrowed loosely from psychology, but the mechanism is different. The model isn't confused or dreaming. It's doing exactly what it was designed to do: predict the most likely next token. The problem is that "likely next token" and "true statement" are not the same thing.

## Why LLMs hallucinate

LLMs are trained to predict text that looks like human writing. Human writing is mostly coherent and factually grounded, so models learn to produce coherent, factually-flavored output. But the model has no internal "truth meter." It has patterns.

Three root causes explain most hallucinations:

**1. Knowledge gaps.** The model wasn't trained on the right data, or the data has a cutoff date. It has to fill the gap somehow — and it fills it with plausible-sounding text.

**2. The prompt pushes toward confidence.** If you ask "What is the GDP of [some place the model knows nothing about]?", the model may answer rather than say "I don't know." Completing the pattern feels natural; refusing feels out of character.

**3. Long-context drift.** In a very long conversation or document, the model's attention becomes diffuse. It may contradict something it said earlier, or blend two facts together in a way that produces a new, wrong claim.

## Three types of hallucination to watch for

| Type | What it looks like | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Factual** | A confident wrong claim about the world | Incorrect dates, names, or specifications |
| **Source fabrication** | Citations or quotes that don't exist | A paper title and author that sound real but aren't |
| **Reasoning drift** | Correct facts, wrong conclusion | "Since A and B, therefore C" — but C doesn't follow |

All three happen in real applications. Source fabrication is particularly costly in anything research-facing. Reasoning drift is the hardest to catch because each individual step can look fine in isolation.

## How to reduce hallucinations

You can't eliminate hallucinations entirely — but you can make them rare enough to ship a product you trust.

### 1. Ground the model with retrieval (RAG)

The single most effective technique. Instead of asking the model to answer from memory, you give it the relevant documents and tell it to answer *from those documents only*.

```text
System: You are a helpful assistant. Answer ONLY using the provided context.
If the answer is not in the context, say "I don't have that information."

Context: [your retrieved documents]

User: [question]
```

A direct instruction to stay within the context, combined with real sources, dramatically cuts factual errors. This is the main reason [RAG](/blog/what-is-rag) was invented in the first place.

### 2. Use structured outputs

When you need specific fields filled in, ask for JSON (or another schema) and validate the result. A model that must output `{"confidence": "high" | "medium" | "low"}` is less likely to invent things than one writing free prose, because the structure constrains the output space.

### 3. Write honest system prompts

Tell the model explicitly what to do when it doesn't know:

```text
If you are not certain, say "I'm not sure" rather than guessing.
Do not cite sources you cannot verify.
```

This sounds obvious, but the default behavior — without explicit instructions — is to fill silence with plausible-sounding text. A direct instruction changes that default.

### 4. Chain-of-thought for reasoning tasks

For tasks that involve multi-step logic, asking the model to reason step by step before giving a final answer forces it to surface its logic. This often catches reasoning drift before it reaches the conclusion. It also makes failures far easier to debug.

### 5. Evaluate, don't just eyeball

The most important technique is also the least glamorous: run evals. Pick a set of questions where you know the right answer, run your pipeline against them, and measure how often it gets things wrong. Without this, you have no idea whether a prompt change made things better or worse.

> Rule of thumb: if you'd be embarrassed to show a user the wrong answer, build an eval that catches it before they see it.

## What a high hallucination rate is telling you

A system that hallucinates often is almost always a **retrieval or prompting problem**, not a model problem. Before switching models, ask:

- Is the relevant information actually in the context? (Retrieval failure — see the [common RAG pitfalls](/blog/rag-what-breaks-in-production))
- Did the prompt give the model an explicit out when it doesn't know? (Prompt design)
- Is the task itself unanswerable from the available data? (Scope problem)

Answering these three questions will fix most hallucination issues in production without touching the model at all.
