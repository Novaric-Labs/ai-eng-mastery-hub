Shipping an LLM feature without evals is like releasing software without tests — and hoping users will tell you when it breaks. **Evals** (short for evaluations) are the discipline of measuring how well your LLM system is actually doing, before and after every change you make.

Most teams skip evals early on. They push a new prompt, break something they didn't know was working, and spend two days tracing what changed. Evals are how you get out of that loop.

## What an eval actually is

An eval is a repeatable test that measures LLM output quality. Every eval has three parts:

1. **An input** — a question, document, or task you hand the model.
2. **An expected output** — what a correct or good response looks like.
3. **A scorer** — something that compares actual output to expected and returns a result.

The scorer is where most of the design work lives. "Correct" is easy when there's one right answer. It's much harder when the question is open-ended — "was that response helpful?" or "did it stay on topic?"

## The three main approaches

### 1. Assertion-based evals

The simplest form: check that the output meets a rule you can express in code.

```python
def test_response_cites_source(response: str, retrieved_chunks: list[str]) -> bool:
    # Fail if the answer references a fact not present in any retrieved chunk
    return any(chunk.strip() in response for chunk in retrieved_chunks)
```

Assertion-based evals work well when:

- You need a specific format (valid JSON, a number in a range, a URL that parses).
- You can define "wrong" without ambiguity (contains forbidden words, exceeds a length limit, missing a required field).

They're cheap, fast, and deterministic. The downside: they can't judge quality, helpfulness, or tone.

### 2. Human evals

A person — you, a teammate, or a hired rater — reads the output and scores it. This is the ground truth. Human judgment is the thing you're ultimately trying to approximate with automation.

Human evals are:

- **Expensive and slow** at any real scale.
- **Essential early on** to build a labeled dataset you can learn from.
- **The only reliable way** to catch subtle failures that automated scorers miss.

A common pattern: run human evals once to label 50–100 examples, then use that labeled set to build and validate an automated scorer. You don't run human evals on every change — you use them to confirm that your automated scorer stays trustworthy.

### 3. LLM-as-judge evals

You use a second LLM call to grade the output. You pass the input, the response, and optionally a reference answer to a separate grading prompt. The judge returns a score and a brief explanation.

| What to grade | Example judge prompt |
|---|---|
| Factual accuracy | "Does the answer contradict the source documents?" |
| Relevance | "Does the answer actually address the user's question?" |
| Tone | "Is this response professional and concise?" |

LLM-as-judge handles open-ended quality dimensions that code can't. It's also noisier — judges hallucinate, too — which is why anchoring them to a clear rubric, and spot-checking with human evals, matters.

> Rule of thumb: **use all three**. Assertions for format and safety, LLM-as-judge for open-ended quality, and human evals to calibrate and catch blind spots.

## Why evals matter more than the model

It's tempting to swap models and see if things "feel better." But without evals, you can't answer:

- Did changing the model actually improve accuracy on real queries?
- Did updating the prompt break something that was working?
- Is retrieval finding the right chunks, or just *some* chunks?

Evals turn those questions into numbers. That's the difference between tuning a system and guessing at one.

## Where to start

You don't need a full evaluation platform on day one. A minimal starting point:

1. **Pick 20–30 real inputs** that represent what your system will actually see in production.
2. **Label the expected outputs** yourself or with a teammate.
3. **Write one assertion** — something that catches a clearly wrong answer.
4. **Add one LLM-as-judge prompt** for the quality dimension that matters most to you.
5. **Run this eval suite after every prompt or retrieval change** and track whether scores go up or down.

Twenty examples won't catch everything. But a small test suite you actually run beats a comprehensive framework you never finish building.

## The evals mindset

Think of evals as the "this passed" signal that lets you ship with confidence — not a research project or a compliance checkbox. Start small, stay practical, and add coverage as your system grows.
